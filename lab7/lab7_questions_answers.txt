probabilistic graphical models - lab 7
hidden markov model

1(a) question: represent the initial, transition, and emission distributions.
answer: the initial distribution is P(F)=0.5 and P(L)=0.5. The transition matrix is:
        F     L
    F  0.95  0.05
    L  0.10  0.90
The fair die emits every roll with probability 1/6. The loaded die emits rolls
1 to 5 with probability 0.10 each and roll 6 with probability 0.50. Every row sums to 1.

1(b) question: what conditional independences does the HMM graph assert?
answer: for each position t, state t points to state t+1 and emits observation t.
Given state t, the past is conditionally independent of the future. The observations
are conditionally independent when all their corresponding hidden states are known.

1(c) question: generate 300 rolls and compare the loaded proportion with the stationary probability.
answer: the generated loaded proportion is 0.3000. The stationary loaded probability is
0.05 / (0.05 + 0.10) = 0.3333.

1(d) question: how many sixes are in the supplied sequence, and is that enough to locate the loaded stretch?
answer: there are 12 sixes. A fair die would be expected to produce 30/6 = 5 sixes.
This difference suggests loading, but counting alone cannot locate the loaded stretch
because it removes the order and position of the rolls.

2(e) question: define the forward variable alpha(t, s).
answer: alpha(t, s) is the joint probability of observations 0 through t and hidden
state s at position t.

2(f) question: state and implement the forward algorithm.
answer: initialise alpha(0,s) = pi(s)P(o0|s). Then use
alpha(t,s) = P(ot|s) sum over r of alpha(t-1,r)P(s|r).
The probability of the complete observation sequence is the sum of the final row.

2(g) question: verify the forward algorithm on five observations.
answer: forward probability = 0.0001091196051955.
Brute-force probability = 0.0001091196051955. They agree to machine precision.

2(h) question: compare operation counts and growth rates.
answer: with S hidden states, direct enumeration has O(S^T) growth. Forward inference
has O(T*S^2) growth. For S=2, the counts printed by the program are:
T=5: direct 32, forward 20.
T=20: direct 1,048,576, forward 80.
T=300: direct 2^300, forward 1,200.

3(i) question: investigate numerical underflow.
answer: the direct forward probability first becomes exactly zero at length 500 for
the generated sequences tested. It is also zero at length 800.

3(j) question: implement the log-space remedy.
answer: log-space stores log probabilities. Products become additions, and sums use
the numerically safe log-sum-exp operation. The log probability is -49.194758298968.

3(k) question: implement the scaling remedy.
answer: after every time step, divide the forward vector by its sum and add the log
of that sum to the accumulated log probability. The result is -49.194758298968,
which agrees with the log-space result.

3(l) question: compare the two numerical remedies.
answer: log-space is very robust and works naturally with extremely small values.
Scaling is often simpler and is convenient for the backward pass because the same
scale factors can be reused. Scaling is used more conveniently with filtering and
smoothing.

4(m) question: define the backward variable and state its recursion.
answer: beta(t,s) is the probability of all observations after position t given that
the hidden state at t is s. Initialise beta(T-1,s)=1. Then recurse backwards:
beta(t,s) = sum over r of P(r|s)P(o(t+1)|r)beta(t+1,r).

4(n) question: compare forward and backward probabilities.
answer: forward probability = 4.315070863090e-22.
Backward probability = 4.315070863090e-22. The two results agree.

4(o) question: calculate the smoothed posterior.
answer: the smoothed posterior is proportional to alpha(t,s)beta(t,s), then normalised
over the two states. The two state probabilities sum to 1 at every position.

4(p) question: distinguish filtering and smoothing.
answer: filtering uses observations from the beginning through the current position.
Smoothing uses the complete sequence, including observations after the current position.
The program prints both loaded-state probabilities for all 30 positions.

4(q) question: where do filtering and smoothing differ most?
answer: they differ most at position 23 (using one-based positions). The smoother knows
the future rolls after position 23, while the filter does not.

5(r) question: compare the Viterbi and forward recursions.
answer: the forward algorithm sums the scores of all predecessor states. Viterbi takes
the maximum predecessor score instead. In log-space, both algorithms use additions,
but forward uses log-sum-exp while Viterbi uses max.

5(s) question: implement Viterbi and report the path and its log probability.
answer: the Viterbi path is:
F F F F F F F F F F F F F F F F F F F L L L L L L L L L L L
The log probability of this path is -52.162126548463.

5(t) question: why is the back-pointer table necessary?
answer: each Viterbi state stores which predecessor produced its best score. Tracing
these back-pointers from the final state gives one consistent best path. Choosing the
best state independently at every position can join incompatible transitions.

5(u) question: evaluate Viterbi on ten generated sequences.
answer: over ten generated sequences of length 300, the mean Viterbi accuracy is 0.7930.

6(v) question: compare Viterbi and posterior decoding on the supplied sequence.
answer: the two decoded paths disagree at 2 positions.

6(w) question: compare both methods over ten generated sequences.
answer: the mean Viterbi accuracy is 0.7930, the mean posterior-decoding accuracy is
0.7823, and the mean number of disagreements is 52.40 positions.

6(x) question: what does each method optimise, and when can posterior decoding be impossible?
answer: Viterbi finds the single most probable complete state sequence. Posterior
decoding chooses the most probable state independently at every position, so it can
have better position-by-position accuracy. If a transition probability is zero,
posterior decoding can choose adjacent states whose transition is impossible, giving
a complete sequence with probability exactly zero.
